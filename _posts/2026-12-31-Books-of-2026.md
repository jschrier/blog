Books I read in 2026...

(When I was in college,  a philosophy professor  mentioned in passing that he kept a log of every book he had read since high school. It seemed like a great idea, but I never took it up.  Now, at the end of each year, I see other people make blog posts about their reading list and consider doing it myself.  In July, I decided to work retroactively to construct the list, and intend to do this going forward....backfilling notes while on the morning train to the office)

- Grove, [High Output Management](https://amzn.to/4fpTTkl) - reread this, in an attempt to up my game and accept an increasingly managerial role.  Also relevant as agentic coding turns us all into managers. A key idea: Think about your job as a manufacturing production, identify the rate limiting steps, define indicators, seek efficiency. As a manager: focus on high leverage tasks, and make 1-on-1s and performance reviews meaningful (they are high leverage if they result in empowered autonomous operators; beyond humans, this suggests the importance of plan generation, etc. in managing LLMs)  
- Paul, [Catapult: Harry and I Build a Siege Weapon](https://amzn.to/4fpTTkl) - recently reissued, and a [NY Times book review intrigued me](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/12/books/review/jim-paul-catapult.html) to check it out.  A fun romp in early 1990s San Francisco Bay Area, in which a mechanically inept journalist teams up with his mechanically adept artist buddy to build a catapult.
- Talbot, [Season of the Witch: Enchanment, Terror, and Deliverance in the City of Love](https://amzn.to/4vFzj5y) - be sure to play the music from the appendix while reading.  History of San Francisco from the 1930s - 1990s heavily focused on the 1960s and 1970s. It is amazing to me how dirty the "martyrs" Harvey Milk and George Moscone were, including close ties to Jim Jone's People's Temple....and how violent  the 1970s were in general.   
- Jennings, [Paradise Now: The Story of American Utopianism](https://amzn.to/4wllGd6) - mentioned in passing in a NY Times book review on a more recent book on american utopianism.  The spiritual ancestors of hippie communes, and how they all failed. Pay a visit to [Shaker period room at the Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/134), the next time you are in NYC.
- de Jouvancy [The Way to Learn and the Way to Teach](https://jesuitsources.bc.edu/the-way-to-learn-and-the-way-to-teach/) - In light of revisions to the core curriculum, development of a STEM Honors program, etc. I've tried to do more deep diving into the history of Jesuit pedagogy.  This manual (with facing latin and english translation) was intended for Jesuits in formation teaching in the equivalent of high schools.  Much of the advice on developing elqouence in Greek may not be so relevant for my own task, but advice on classroom management and balancing the role of teacher and learner is of perenial value.  If you're into this type of thing from the perspective of practical considerations for the modern age, I'd recommend [Claude Pavur's book on Ratio Studiorum, *In the School of Ignatius: Studious Zeal and Devoted Learning](https://amzn.to/4yT1CAu) instead.
- Buford, [Among The Thugs](https://amzn.to/4bcSQmB) - The World Cup was in NYC this year, and whenever anyone asked me about it my standard response was: "I don't care about football, but let me tell you about this book on English football hooligans of the early 90s...".  I tremendously enjoyed the book. I had picked it up on a whim to read during flight back from SFO, and broke into hysterical laugher several times, perplexing by vibe-coding aislemates.  
- Bloom, [Developing Talent in Young People](https://amzn.to/4bCUzla) - a cross-section study of high performing individuals in various fields (concert piano, tennis, swimming, neuroscience, math, sculpting) and how they got there.  Some commonalities: (i) Have parents that care; (ii) Encourage your children to get started with whatever local teacher or experience is possible; (iii) Rely on the teachers to help shape the next progression; (iv) At some point, you have to focus on one thing; (v) There are transitional stages and plateaus.  With regards to the math and neuroscience---while both groups often had childhood experiences with science kits and other experiments, the mathematicians tended to have experiences that emphasized first principles and figuring it out for yourself, whereas the neuroscientists had more structured/supervised activities.
- Barett, [An Introduction to Optical Art](https://amzn.to/4sD2Ueo) - more [extensive notes in an earlier post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-04-16-Cyril-Barett,-SJ %})
- [Electric Dreams: Art and Technology Before the Internet](https://amzn.to/426bKGQ) - more [extensive notes in an earlier post]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-05-07-Electric-Dreams:-Art and Technology-Before-The-Internet %})
- Geiger, [Chapel of Extreme Experience: A Short History of Stroboscopic Light and the Dream Machine (2003)](https://archive.org/details/chapelofextremee0000geig) - out of print, but in the Fordham library; came across this via [Electric Dreams]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-05-07-Electric-Dreams:-Art and Technology-Before-The-Internet %}). A short and strange little nonfiction book about an electromechanical hallucination machine. 
- Nahin, [Oliver Heaviside: The Life, Work, and Times of an Electrical Genius of the Victorian Age](https://amzn.to/3RsxGKk) - Enjoyable read from the early oevre of Paul Nahin, with a good compromise between biographical color and mathematical/physical detail.  This came across my radar when thinking about impedance spectroscopy with a student.
- Gies & Gies, [Life in a Medieval City](https://amzn.to/3TvSfpX) -  I have a fondness for "everyday life in X" (where X = [Ancient Rome](https://amzn.to/4yOelUU), Japan, etc.) type books. This is a less-scholarly book, set in roughly the 1300s which gives a flavor of life at all levels.  
- Holmes, [Daily Living in the Twelfth Century: Based on Observation so Alexander Neckam in London and Paris](https://amzn.to/4b3wyDK) - a more scholarly version, roughly 1250, following a specific clerical traveler (based on his notebooks), but still highly readable and entertaining.  
- Lowney, [Heroic Leadership: Best Practices from a 450-year-old Company that Changed the World](https://amzn.to/4ftwaB9) - leadership lessons from the history of the Jesuits.  Could be useful for a third or fouth year [STEM Honors](https://www.fordham.edu/fordham-college-at-rose-hill/honors-programs-societies-and-awards/stem-honors-program/) seminar.
- Briscoe & Jancyzk, [Saint Dominic's Way of Life: A Path to Knowing and Loving God](https://amzn.to/4wQOKsq) - picked this up this slim volume while at Notre Dame, and it gave me a new appreciation of Saint Dominic's charism of study, preaching, and chastity. 
- Philips et al, [Physical Biology of the Cell, 2nd Ed](https://amzn.to/4fP9j1V) - an impressive tome, and covers a superset of the material in [Nelson's Physical Models of Living Systems](https://www.physics.upenn.edu/biophys/PMLS2e/index.html).  I particularly like the section on the stat mech treatment of ligand binding (which elegantly derives the Hill function).  However, the material is much too expansive to be covered in a single semester (albeit, there is cool stuff I want to study myself, such as mechanical and ionic properties) and I found the computational materials to be unevenly distributed across chapters.  
- Bornet, [Irreplaceable: The Art of Standing Out in the Age of Artificial Intelligence](https://amzn.to/4yy1OVg) - was recommended to me by [Konstantinos Vogiatzis](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=4ivGxSoAAAAJ&hl=en&oi=ao) during a visit to UTK this past Spring.  I found the style off putting (reads like an LLM-generated listicle), but there are some good ideas buried inside: specifically: invest in *humic* skills (genuine creativity, social authenticity, and critical thinking), identify ways to automate tasks using AI to free up time for this, beware of manipulation by social media.
- Knaflic, [Storytelling with data: a data visualization guide for business professionals](https://amzn.to/4w0PM4q) - I'm teaching a seminar on data visualizations and was looking for something less philosophical and more practical than Tufte. Saw this in the Notre Dame campus bookstore, and upon closer examination this seems like a worthy replacement to use in my course.  What I like was the emphasis on storytelling and discussion of various Gestalt psychological principles that can be used to make effective charts.  
- Schwabish, [Better Data Visualizations: A Guide for Scholars, Researchers, and Wonks](https://amzn.to/4h0smZf) - Another contender for my course. This has lots of pretty chart types and discussions of how they can work well or fail (and also covers the various Gestalt principles, etc.), but is a bit too verbose and lengthy for my purposes.
- Brand, [Maintence of Everything: Part One](https://amzn.to/4wjoffq) - Beautifully written and typeset book about the importance of maintenance, revisiting some of my favorites like [The Long Way](https://amzn.to/4ffAYd8), [Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance](https://amzn.to/4wULgoV), [Shopcraft as Soulcraft](https://amzn.to/4htcEWx), and [Masters of Precision](https://amzn.to/4ba6t61)
- Eliaz and Gileadi, [Physical Electrochemistry: Fundamentals, Techniques, and Applications 2nd ed](https://amzn.to/4vBHtfn) - I got into an electrochemistry kick by hanging out with [Cole Gruninger](https://colegruninger.com) and hacking improved [HTML5](https://jschrier.github.io/potentiostat/software/javascript/app/webserial-app.html) and [Mathematica interfaces](https://github.com/jschrier/rodeostat_wolfram) to the [Rodeostat potentiostat](https://blog.iorodeo.com/rodeostat-introduction/). Really nice text, and I appreciated the in-depth coverage of corrosion and electrochemical deposition.
- Honeychurch, [Simulating electrochemical reactions with Mathematica (2006)](https://amzn.to/4fwlIaR) - some good ideas, but based on older version of Mathematica. Skip the paper book and go with [his updated notebooks and text on Github](https://github.com/mikeh1980/Simulating-Electrochemical-Reactions-with-Mathematica). Even those are a bit dated to my tastes, and I spent some time rewriting these to my own style (perhaps one day I shall post). Honeychurch spends a fair amount of time implementing different finite difference methods (which is fine and educational), but a notable omission is the (more recent) [finite element support in Mathematica](http://reference.wolfram.com/language/FEMDocumentation/guide/FiniteElementMethodGuide.html), which I admittedly have very limited experience with. 
- Alon, [Systems Medicine](https://amzn.to/3TdqFOb) - I had this on my shelf for 2 years, with the intent of using material in my biochemical modeling course, starting it a few times, but never got into it.  Finally, I brought it to read during a vacation in rural Canada.  Engaging read about biological feedback loops in human health/disease/aging, culminating in a periodic table of disease processes arising from the ways that evolutionarily appropriate feedback mechanisms can fail.
- McNeill & Freiberger [Fuzzy Logic: The Revolutionary Computer Technology That is Changing Our World (1994)](https://amzn.to/4wfd3jW) - As a graduate student at Berkeley in the early 2000s, I spent some time hanging around the electrical engineering department, where undergraduates spoke of [Zadeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotfi_A._Zadeh) with whispered awe directed mostly at the [z-transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-transform).  Fuzzy logic seems like a great-for-the-limits-of-the time technology, that has gone extinct because of Sutton's [Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html). (But hey, [I like negronis](https://crayonfou.com/products/the-bitter-lesson)...) Yet, despite being a technical dead end, I find something appealing about it; perhaps this is the equivalent of learning to write elegant verse in ancient Assyrian. A fun aspect of reading  popularization books about emerging compute technologies from 30-40 years ago is seeing the same breathless optimism about the immenent deployment of AI, natural language communication, atc. that the popular press and socials spew today about our technology du jour (LLM). There is no new hype train under the sun. 
- Douady et al, [Do Plants Know Math?: Unwinding the Story of Plant Spirals, from Leonardo da Vinci to Now](https://amzn.to/4wF8nnU) - Better titled "can humans use math to explain [phyllotaxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllotaxis)"
